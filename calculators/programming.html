Homeowner Questionnaire

For the start of a residential project — fill this in before the first working session with your architect. There are no wrong answers, and nothing here commits you to anything. Where two of your answers pull against each other, leave them both: the collisions are the most useful thing you can hand your architect.

Answers save in this browser as you type. When you're done, print this page to PDF or copy a text summary to email back.

1 · The project, in your words
Your words, not drawing words. "Make the back of the house work for a family that lives in the kitchen" beats "addition."
A baby, a sale, working from home, a retirement, a repair that grew. Whatever set this in motion shapes the priorities more than any wishlist.
A spouse or partner, kids, a parent helping fund it, an HOA board. List everyone whose yes matters, even informally.
2 · The practical frame

These set the box the design lives inside. It can feel backwards to talk numbers before rooms — it isn't. Knowing the box first is what lets the design spend your money where you care most.

A range is fine. Construction almost always needs a contingency on top — typically 10–20% held in reserve — so a number with that already in mind is worth more than a hopeful one.
A lease ending, a school year, a wedding, a birth. "Soon" is fine too — just say which kind of soon.
What do you already have or know? Check what applies:
— the drawing that shows your legal lot lines
— matters early if bathrooms or bedrooms are involved
A tree you'd never remove, a view you bought the house for, a neighbor situation, known problems (water, settling, knob-and-tube).
3 · How you live

Describe activities, not rooms. "We host twelve for dinner a few times a year" tells your architect more than "big dining room" — it might not even be a dining room that solves it.

Cooking together or one cook? Homework at the counter? Two people on video calls at once? Instruments, gear, a dog that needs washing?
"Four daily, ten at Thanksgiving, kids' friends most Fridays."
Bedroom away from the street side; laundry near bedrooms; office where the doorbell can't end a meeting.
Where do shoes, bags, mail, and the dog leash actually land? Where does the day bottleneck? This finds the rooms no one thinks to ask for.
4 · Need · want · dream

List everything, then mark each one. Need — the project fails without it. Want — real, but you'd trade it. Dream — if the budget smiles. Be stingy with Need: when everything is a need, nothing is. And don't pre-trim the dreams — they tell your architect what the project is really about.

5 · Comfort, character, and the long run
Drafty-room grudges, a too-hot bedroom, utility bills, solar someday, quiet. Say it even if you don't know the technical name for it.
A bedroom and full bath that could live on the main floor, wider doorways, a step-free way in. Planning this now is cheap; retrofitting it isn't.
"We'll oil the deck every year" and "we never will" are both fine answers — they lead to different materials.
Links, magazine pages, photos of places you've been. The one you hate is as useful as the three you love. A sentence on why, for each, if you can.
6 · Before the next session

Things worth gathering now — each one saves a week later. Check what you can bring; note what you can't find.

Write them down now — first meetings eat memory.
